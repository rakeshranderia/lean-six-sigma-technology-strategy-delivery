# 2. Master Technology Lifecycle

## The lifecycle

The canonical lifecycle in this framework is:

**Business Need → Strategy → Roadmap → Portfolio → Program → Project → Operations → Benefits**

The sequence is deliberately broader than a project lifecycle.

Technology investment begins before a project exists and continues after a project closes.

![Master lifecycle](../diagrams/master-lifecycle.svg)

---

## Business Need

The starting point can be:

- a customer problem;
- a cost problem;
- service instability;
- growth constraints;
- cybersecurity exposure;
- regulatory change;
- data quality;
- poor user experience;
- technical debt;
- market opportunity;
- merger or acquisition;
- capability deficiency.

The important distinction is between **need** and **solution**.

“We need a new CRM” is already a solution statement.

“We cannot maintain a reliable view of customer interactions across sales, service and marketing” is a problem/capability statement.

The second leaves room for evidence and analysis.

---

## Strategy

Strategy determines:

- what outcomes matter;
- which capabilities are important;
- which principles will guide decisions;
- which constraints must be respected;
- where technology will differentiate;
- where standardisation is preferable;
- what target state is desirable.

DMAIC overlap:

- **Define** strategic problem/opportunity.
- **Measure** current capability/maturity.
- **Analyse** gap and systemic causes.

---

## Roadmap

A roadmap converts strategy into sequence.

A strong roadmap shows more than dates.

It should explain:

- the current state;
- the target state;
- capability gaps;
- major dependencies;
- sequencing logic;
- enabling work;
- expected benefits;
- investment horizons;
- decision gates.

DMAIC overlap:

- late **Analyse**;
- **Improve** design and sequencing;
- early **Control** through roadmap governance.

---

## Portfolio

Portfolio management decides where scarce resources go.

Typical constraints include:

- capital;
- operating budget;
- specialist capability;
- change capacity;
- vendor capacity;
- business absorption;
- regulatory deadlines;
- technical dependencies.

Lean Six Sigma contributes:

- prioritisation against measurable outcomes;
- Pareto-style focus;
- cost-of-poor-quality thinking;
- risk prioritisation;
- evidence-based stop/start/continue decisions.

The key portfolio question is:

> **Are we investing in the right things, not merely delivering the things already approved?**

---

## Program

Programs coordinate related projects and changes around an outcome.

A program might include:

- technology implementation;
- process redesign;
- data migration;
- integration;
- training;
- operating-model changes;
- vendor transition;
- security uplift.

The program can be “green” from a project-status perspective while the intended business process remains unimproved.

DMAIC forces the program back to the original outcome.

Example:

**Program objective:** reduce customer onboarding time from ten days to two.

The individual projects may deliver:

- new CRM workflow;
- digital identity checks;
- integration;
- data cleansing;
- training.

Project completion is not the final measure.

The onboarding outcome is.

---

## Project

Projects remain essential.

DMAIC strengthens the project front end and back end.

Before delivery:

- define the problem;
- baseline performance;
- analyse causes;
- establish benefit measures.

During delivery:

- test improvement hypotheses;
- track assumptions;
- validate that solution design addresses root causes.

After delivery:

- transition to operations;
- measure outcome;
- confirm ownership;
- sustain control.

---

## Operations

Operations is where improvement either survives or decays.

Control may include:

- service ownership;
- KPIs;
- monitoring;
- alerting;
- runbooks;
- automated controls;
- periodic review;
- architecture standards;
- lifecycle management;
- change governance.

This is why “project close” and “improvement complete” are not the same event.

---

## Benefits

Benefits complete the evidence chain.

A benefit should have:

- an original baseline;
- a target;
- an owner;
- a measurement method;
- a time horizon;
- a review cadence.

Benefits can include:

- cost reduction;
- risk reduction;
- service improvement;
- faster cycle time;
- reduced manual work;
- increased adoption;
- higher conversion;
- improved availability;
- reduced defects;
- stronger recovery performance.

Benefits should feed the next strategy/portfolio cycle.

This closes the loop:

**Benefits → Learning → Strategy → Roadmap → Investment**
